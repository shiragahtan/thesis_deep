\section{Motivation and Thesis Statement}

\subsection{Background: AlphaEvolve}

AlphaEvolve~\cite{alphaevolve2025} (Google DeepMind, 2025) is an evolutionary
program-search framework that uses large language models (LLMs) as mutation
operators. Starting from a seed program, AlphaEvolve iteratively proposes
improved variants using Gemini, evaluated on a fixed benchmark. Its most
notable result was discovering a matrix multiplication algorithm that beat
Strassen's 56-year-old record: reducing complexity from $O(n^{2.807})$ to
$O(n^{2.778})$.

AlphaEvolve's mutation step can be written as:
\[
  x_{t+1} = \mathrm{LLM}(x_t)
\]

The LLM sees only the current code $x_t$. It is \textbf{blind to why the
program failed}: it does not receive the score, the error trace, or any
signal about what went wrong.

\subsection{The Thesis Contribution}

This thesis proposes one targeted upgrade: provide the LLM with \emph{failure
evidence} $E(x_t)$ before each mutation:

\[
  \boxed{x_{t+1} = \mathrm{LLM}\!\left(x_t,\; E(x_t),\; \mathrm{score}(x_t)\right)}
\]

where $E(x_t)$ is a structured failure report containing:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The current score (0--100)
  \item Correctness: how many test cases passed
  \item Speed: measured speedup vs baseline
  \item The last exception message (if any)
  \item A truncated stack trace (if any)
\end{itemize}

The hypothesis is that this \textbf{diagnosis-driven mutation} --- which we
call the \emph{smart step} --- should reach a target fitness with fewer
evaluator calls than the blind mutation (\emph{dumb step}), improving
\emph{sample efficiency}.

We call the blind baseline the \textit{dumb step}:
\[
  x_{t+1} = \mathrm{Mutate\_random}(x_t)
\]

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Research Questions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item[\textbf{Q1}] \textbf{Sample efficiency}: Does failure-informed mutation
    reach target fitness with fewer evaluator calls?
  \item[\textbf{Q2}] \textbf{Explore vs exploit}: When in the search trajectory
    does the smart step help most --- early exploration or late refinement?
  \item[\textbf{Q3}] \textbf{Smart/dumb balance}: What is the optimal ratio of
    smart to dumb steps, especially when diversity collapses?
  \item[\textbf{Q4}] \textbf{LLM convergence}: Does using the same LLM for all
    mutations cause the population to converge to homogeneous code patterns?
  \item[\textbf{Q5}] \textbf{Failure signal}: Which component of $E(x_t)$ is
    most informative --- error type, score delta, or stack trace?
\end{enumerate}

\texttt{population.py}      & Population of programs; weighted parent selection \\
\texttt{mutator.py}         & Smart step (LLM) and dumb step (random + structural) \\
\texttt{evaluators.py}      & Multiple scoring variants (combined, correctness, speed) \\
\texttt{scheduler.py}       & Decides smart vs dumb per generation \\
\texttt{loop.py}            & Main evolutionary loop; saves JSON logs \\
\texttt{main.py}            & CLI entry point \\
\texttt{compare.py}         & Ablation runner: smart vs dumb vs mixed \\

\texttt{plot.py}            & Three-panel results visualization \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection{The Evolutionary Loop}

Each generation:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Select a parent from the top-$K$ programs (weighted by score)
  \item Decide: smart step or dumb step (via Scheduler)
  \item Generate $k$ candidate mutations in parallel (multiple proposers)
  \item Evaluate all candidates on the fixed benchmark
  \item Accept the best candidate if it beats the worst in the population
  \item Log and optionally early-stop if target score is reached
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Dumb Step Mutations}

The dumb step applies one of eight random mutations without any knowledge of
why the program failed:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Swap two adjacent lines
  \item Replace a numeric literal with a nearby value ($\pm 1, \pm 2$)
  \item Swap a comparison or arithmetic operator
  \item Duplicate a line
  \item Delete a non-essential line
  \item \textbf{Insert a sorting primitive} (insertion sort, merge sort, quicksort, heapsort)
  \item \textbf{Add an early-exit guard} (\texttt{if len(arr) <= 1: return})
  \item \textbf{Rename a loop variable} (tests population diversity)
\end{enumerate}

Items 6--8 are \emph{structural mutations} added to make the dumb-step
baseline stronger --- a better baseline makes the comparison with the smart
step more meaningful.

\subsection{Evaluator Variants}

Four scoring functions are implemented in \texttt{evaluators.py}:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lp{8cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Variant} & \textbf{Formula} \\
\midrule
\texttt{combined}      & $70\% \times \text{correctness} + 30\% \times \text{speed}$ (default) \\
\texttt{correctness}   & $100\% \times \text{correctness}$, speed ignored \\
\texttt{speed}         & $100\% \times \text{speed}$, hard gate on correctness \\
\texttt{strict\_speed} & $\min(100,\; 20 \times \text{speedup})$, finer resolution \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\section{Benchmark Tasks}

We test on four benchmark tasks spanning different algorithmic domains.
Each task has a correct-but-slow seed program and a well-known fast target,
so the LLM's ability to read failure evidence and propose a better algorithm
is directly testable.

The general scoring formula is:
\[
  \text{score} = w_c \cdot \frac{\text{correct}}{\text{total}} + \min\!\left(w_s,\; w_s \cdot \frac{t_{\text{baseline}}}{t_{\text{candidate}}}\right)
\]

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llllccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Task} & \textbf{Function} & \textbf{Seed} & \textbf{Target} & \textbf{Seed score} & $w_c$ & $w_s$ \\
\midrule
Sorting        & \texttt{sort\_array(arr)}     & Selection sort $O(n^2)$      & Merge/Quicksort $O(n\log n)$ & 70.8 & 70 & 30 \\
Matrix mult.   & \texttt{matmul(A, B)}         & Naive $O(n^3)$               & Strassen-like               & 95.3 & 60 & 40 \\
Prime sieve    & \texttt{get\_primes(n)}       & Trial division $O(n\sqrt{n})$ & Sieve of Eratosthenes        & 74.8 & 70 & 30 \\
String search  & \texttt{find\_pattern(t, p)}  & Naive $O(nm)$                & KMP / built-in find         & 73.1 & 70 & 30 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection{Sorting}

\textbf{Seed}: Selection sort — $O(n^2)$, always correct, very slow.
\textbf{Baseline}: Python's built-in \texttt{sorted()} (Timsort, implemented in C).
\textbf{Test cases}: 20 fixed arrays (seed 42), sizes 10--200, values $[-1000, 1000]$.

The starting score of 70.8 reflects full correctness (70 pts) but almost no speed
points (selection sort is $\sim$50$\times$ slower than Timsort). The LLM,
reading ``speed: 0.02$\times$ baseline'', should reason towards merge sort or quicksort.

\subsection{Matrix Multiplication}

\textbf{Seed}: Naive $O(n^3)$ triple-loop multiplication.
\textbf{Baseline}: Same naive algorithm (the seed \emph{is} the baseline here — the
interesting improvement is discovering Strassen-like sub-cubic algorithms).
\textbf{Test cases}: 15 cases ($5 \times$ each of $2\times2$, $4\times4$, $8\times8$ matrices).

This task directly mirrors AlphaEvolve's headline result (beating Strassen after
56 years). Our framework tests whether a free LLM can rediscover known improvements.

\subsection{Prime Number Generation}

\textbf{Seed}: Trial division — $O(n\sqrt{n})$, correct but slow.
\textbf{Baseline}: Sieve of Eratosthenes — $O(n \log \log n)$.
\textbf{Test cases}: 10 fixed upper bounds $N \in [100, 5000]$ (seed 42).

Starting score 74.8 (correct but $\sim$6$\times$ slower than the sieve).
The LLM failure signal ``speed: 0.16$\times$ baseline'' should motivate
switching to the sieve structure.

\subsection{Substring Search}

\textbf{Seed}: Brute-force substring scan — $O(nm)$ per search.
\textbf{Baseline}: Python's built-in \texttt{str.find()} (Boyer-Moore-Horspool in C).
\textbf{Test cases}: 15 fixed (text, pattern) pairs; text lengths 200--2000, pattern 3--15 chars.

Starting score 73.1 (correct but $\sim$10$\times$ slower than \texttt{str.find}).
The LLM should propose KMP or similar prefix-table approaches.

After adding structural mutations (insert sorting primitive: quicksort, merge sort,
heap sort, insertion sort), the dumb step became stronger:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Seed} & \textbf{Start} & \textbf{Final} & \textbf{Evals} & \textbf{Best algorithm found} \\

\textbf{Key observations}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Even with structural mutations that can ``insert quicksort'', the dumb
    step cannot go beyond 74.2. The structural mutation is random — it cannot
    \emph{reason} about why the algorithm is slow and choose the right fix.
  \item The population diversity collapses to near 0 by generation 20 in all runs.
  \item \textbf{The target score of 95 is never reached by dumb-only},
    regardless of seeds or structural mutation variants.
  \item The improvement from 71.1 to 74.2 (between old and new dumb step) is
    entirely due to the lucky insertion of quicksort — not any reasoning.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Smart-Only vs Dumb-Only — First Comparison (Sorting)}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccl}
\toprule
\textbf{Config} & \textbf{Final Score} & \textbf{Evaluations} & \textbf{Best algorithm found} \\
\midrule
Dumb-only & 74.2 & 100 & Quicksort (iterative, by lucky mutation) \\
Smart-only & 71.9 & 101 & Shell sort + Sedgewick gaps + insertion sort hybrid \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Interpretation — why dumb beat smart on sorting:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The dumb step's structural mutation library includes pre-written correct
    implementations of quicksort, merge sort, heap sort, and insertion sort.
    It can ``accidentally'' insert the right algorithm without any reasoning.
  \item The smart step proposes \emph{more sophisticated} algorithms (Shell sort
    with Sedgewick gaps, hybrid approaches), but pure-Python implementations of
    these algorithms are slower than the pre-written iterative quicksort primitive.
  \item The scoring signal on the sorting task is extremely hard to improve:
    the baseline is Python's \texttt{sorted()} (Timsort, implemented in C).
    No pure-Python algorithm can realistically approach 100/100.
    This ``ceiling effect'' means both strategies plateau.
  \item \textbf{The sorting benchmark may not be the best showcase for the smart
    step.} The advantage of failure-informed mutation should be clearer on tasks
    where the LLM must reason structurally (e.g.\ prime sieve, string search)
    rather than compete with a C-implemented baseline.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{What the smart step \emph{did} demonstrate:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item In generation 1, the LLM immediately proposed merge sort — recognising from
    the failure evidence (``speed: 0.02$\times$ baseline, correct but $O(n^2)$'')
    that the algorithm needs to be fundamentally different.
  \item By the final generation, the LLM had explored Shell sort, insertion sort
    hybrids, and gap-sequence optimisations — all semantically meaningful responses
    to the failure signal. The dumb step never ``understood'' why the code was slow.
  \item The smart step maintained non-zero diversity (div=0.4) throughout all 50
    generations, while dumb-only collapsed to div=0 by generation 15.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Smart-Only vs Dumb-Only — Prime Number Generation}

This benchmark is the clearest demonstration of the thesis contribution.
The dumb step has no sieve primitive to insert — it is structurally blind.
The smart step, reading the failure evidence, can reason towards the sieve.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccccl}
\toprule
\textbf{Config} & \textbf{Score} & \textbf{Evals} & \textbf{Time} & \textbf{Target?} & \textbf{Best algorithm} \\
\midrule
Dumb-only  & 75.3  & 77 & 0.2s  & \texttimes & Trial division + cosmetic edits \\
Smart-only & \textbf{100.0} & \textbf{9} & 44s & \checkmark (gen 4) & Sieve of Eratosthenes (bytearray) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Key result}: The smart step achieved \textbf{perfect score (100/100) in 9 evaluations}
— the dumb step was stuck at 75.3 after 77 evaluations.
This is an $\mathbf{8.5\times}$ improvement in sample efficiency.

The LLM-generated solution:
\begin{lstlisting}
def get_primes(n: int) -> list:
    """Sieve of Eratosthenes - O(n log log n)."""
    if n < 2:
        return []
    sieve = bytearray(b'\x01') * (n + 1)
    sieve[0:2] = b'\x00\x00'
    for i in range(2, int(n**0.5) + 1):
        if sieve[i]:
            sieve[i*i::i] = bytearray(len(sieve[i*i::i]))
    return [i for i, is_p in enumerate(sieve) if is_p]
\end{lstlisting}

The LLM read ``speed: 0.17$\times$ sieve baseline'' and immediately proposed the
Sieve of Eratosthenes — with a \texttt{bytearray} optimisation for memory
efficiency. The dumb step made only cosmetic edits (duplicate lines, perturbed
constants) and never discovered the fundamentally different algorithm.

\textbf{Observations}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Sorting, Primes, Strings}: dumb-only consistently plateaus between

  \item \textbf{Matrix}: reaches 95+ in 2--5 evaluations regardless of seed.

  \item The three meaningful benchmarks (sorting, primes, strings) show
    that \textbf{the dumb step has a hard ceiling near 75/100}, set by the
    limits of random syntactic mutation without reasoning.
\end{itemize}

The LLM-generated solution:
\begin{lstlisting}
def find_pattern(text: str, pattern: str) -> list:
    """Optimized: use built-in str.find in a loop for efficient search."""
    results = []
    n, m = len(text), len(pattern)
    if m == 0:
        return []
    start = 0
    while True:
        idx = text.find(pattern, start)
        if idx == -1:
            break
        results.append(idx)
        start = idx + 1
    return results
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{Interpretation}: The LLM read the failure evidence
(``speed: 0.09$\times$ baseline, correct but slow'') and reasoned that
Python's built-in \texttt{str.find()} is implemented in C and should
be used instead of a pure-Python character-by-character loop.
This is exactly the kind of \emph{semantic reasoning} the thesis proposes:
the smart step identified \emph{why} the code was slow and chose the
appropriate tool. The dumb step made only cosmetic edits
(variable renames, off-by-one tweaks) and never escaped the O(nm) basin.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Sweep (Dumb-Only, 36 Configs)}

We ran a grid search over population size, number of proposers, and top-$K$
parents — all in dumb-only mode (no LLM). This gives us the best configuration
to use as a baseline before introducing the smart step.

\textbf{Parameter grid:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{POPULATION\_SIZE} $\in \{5, 10, 20\}$
  \item \texttt{NUM\_PROPOSERS} $\in \{1, 3, 5, 10\}$
  \item \texttt{TOP\_K\_PARENTS} $\in \{1, 3, 5\}$
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Key finding}: The number of proposers matters most. With 1 proposer
per generation, all configs plateau at 70.5 regardless of population size or
top-$K$. With $\geq 3$ proposers, scores reach 71.7--71.9. The best
configuration found:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
\textbf{Config} & \textbf{Score} & \textbf{Evaluations} \\
\midrule
pop=10, proposers=10, top-k=1 & 71.91 & 94 \\
pop=20, proposers=10, top-k=3 & 71.90 & 99 \\
pop=10, proposers=5,  top-k=1 & 71.90 & 51 \\
\midrule
\textit{1 proposer (all configs)} & $\approx$70.5 & 11 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Interpretation}: More proposers = better coverage of the mutation space
per generation. But even the best dumb-only config is stuck at 71.9 — confirming
the baseline cannot escape the $O(n^2)$ basin without semantic guidance.

For the smart-step comparison, we will use the balanced default:
\texttt{pop=10, proposers=5, top-k=3}.

is correct), making this a trivial task. The \texttt{speed} and
\texttt{strict\_speed} evaluators give nearly 0 (selection sort is 50$\times$
slower than Python's built-in Timsort), making the search objective pure
speed optimization. This validates all four variants behave as intended.

\subsection{Matrix Benchmark Sanity Check}

The naive $O(n^3)$ seed program on the matrix task:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Score: 97.8/100
  \item Correct: 15/15 test cases
  \item Speedup: 0.95$\times$ vs baseline (essentially identical --- baseline \emph{is} naive)
\end{itemize}

The high score means the matrix task is less challenging at the baseline level.
The interesting question is whether the search can discover Strassen-like
divide-and-conquer approaches that go \emph{faster} than naive.

\subsection{Engineering Robustness}

During the sweep, we discovered and fixed a critical bug: the \texttt{evaluate()}
function accepted a \texttt{timeout} parameter but did not enforce it. Mutations
that introduced infinite loops (e.g.\ \texttt{\_swap\_operator} replacing
\texttt{<} with \texttt{>} in a while-loop condition) would hang the process
indefinitely.

\textbf{Fix}: Added \texttt{signal.SIGALRM} enforcement — any evaluation that
exceeds 5 seconds is killed and scored 0. This is now verified:

\begin{lstlisting}
# Infinite-loop program
bad = "def sort_array(arr):\n    while True: pass"
r = evaluate(bad, timeout=2)
# -> score=0.0, error="Timeout after 2.0s (infinite loop?)"
\end{lstlisting}
